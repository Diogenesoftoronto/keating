“Let me predict”, “let me change it”, “let me fix it”

Claude Fable · November 18, 2026 · Keating web 4.2.0 and the local follow-up checkout

A learner who asks to do something should get a control for doing it, without having to name a widget and without a page of prose in front of it. This guide shows how often that happened in scripted tests, what changed, and how to find the next miss.

Final review. Two tutors answered four learner requests with Jev, Gemini Decision and ordinary unguided generation. In two guided turns the tutor drafted a usable prediction field that the learner never saw. In a separate live classroom turn, changing a flash-rate slider from 6 to 7 updated the 12-second total from 72 to 84.   • Earlier four-turn policy check. Three turns returned a reply and each one let the learner do what they asked. One was a slider and two were editable Python. The fourth timed out, so its result is unknown.

What the first Python drafts looked like

Both first drafts used a five-item list and a loop starting at index 1, which silently skips the first item. Neither carried a comment pointing at the bug. How to use the decision models

Each model answers the same 39 typed questions about a turn and Keating turns the scores into a plan. Four things follow from these twelve answers.

  • Trust the specific questions across vendors. The score that picks the kind cleared 0.6 on both models for all four requests. A prediction opportunity scored 0.83 and 0.89 on Jev and 0.751 and 0.872 on Gemini. A hands-on request scored 0.87 and 0.93 on Jev and 0.944 and 0.849 on Gemini.
  • Calibrate the general gate for each model. learning_task ran lower on Gemini than on Jev in all five subject-matter cases, by 0.06 to 0.44. The pairs were 0.79 and 0.663, 0.95 and 0.771, 0.84 and 0.778, 0.68 and 0.356, 0.83 and 0.392. Both scored the account request at 0.02.
  • Read the scores before the offer. An empty offer can hide three strong specific scores behind one weak general one. Policy v2.2 acts on this for variable exploration.
  • Treat an offer as a menu. Jev listed three kinds for the ramp cars. Gemini added Fieldwork for the resistor after scoring outside observation as yes. The tutor is told to use at most one.

Both models marked a learner response as due on the two predictions only, at 0.93 and 0.97 for Jev and 0.927 and 0.917 for Gemini. No other case passed 0.33.

One plan deserves a tutor test. On the hexagon fact, Jev scored learning_task at 0.83, so its prompt held “Give the answer in the requested form and stop” beside the general “Choose one useful next learning step based on demonstrated understanding, then let the learner respond.” No tutor acted on it, so the effect of the conflict is unknown.

How many seconds a learner waited

The diagnostic record has a field for this, replyElapsedMs. It runs from the learner’s message entering an agent run until the released or withheld public reply ends. Rendering comes after it. A retry starts its own clock.

Archived recordings lack the field, so the inspector reports their timing as unknown. No browser timing from a learner appears on this page.

The clock was checked locally against the real agent loop under a controlled clock. It recorded 1.25 seconds for the reply, and settling that finished five seconds later left that figure alone. That shows the field measures what it says. It is no measurement of a tutor.

The saved receipts carry a different clock, the offline wall time of each scripted run. In the first fresh check, MiniMax’s three turns took about 7, 14 and 10 seconds with one draft each. The four release-audit turns that lost their control used three drafts and took about 21 to 80 seconds, including one extra probe call per turn. In the final cohort the one hard number is the 60-second timeout.

Whether the planner’s suggestion helped

Jev named a specific kind on 9 of the 16 action requests in the release audit. Three final replies used an offered kind. The inspector shows 3/13 because Jev also offered a Question on all four plain-text requests, where both tutors rightly ignored it.

A small paired test removed only the sentence that names a kind. With the sentence, 4 of 8 first drafts held a valid control. Without it, 4 of 8 did. A September comparison of the full instruction found a valid control on 6 of 14 matched cases with Jev and 9 of 14 with a compact baseline.

The final Python turns point the same way. The tutor delivered the editor whether or not the plan named it. These comparisons give little reason to make an offer compulsory, and the application keeps it as advice.

How the passive cue label reads wording

A small set of English patterns labels each learner message for the dashboard. The label plays no part in what the tutor does, since the decision model reads the message for that. A missed label hides a turn from the missed-cue view and shrinks its denominator.

I wrote 26 ordinary rewordings of “let me predict” and “I want to change this and see what happens”. The original patterns fired on 2. The current ones fire on 24. The two left alone describe the subject and name no action by the learner, such as “what happens when the rate doubles?” That makes 24 of 26 a detection count on my own phrasings. It is no estimate of recall.

Three sentences were wrongly labelled after that widening. Each is now excluded by a rule about its shape.

SentenceRuleStill labelled
“Let me guess, you want me to memorize it”A guess stated straight after the comma, colon or dash is a remark. A sequencing word keeps the request.“Let me guess first” · “Let me guess, then tell me if I’m right”
“What if I lower my expectations?”A bare what-if about something of the learner’s own is left alone.“What if I lower the rate?” · “I want to adjust my deposit and see what happens to the balance”
“I want to play with the dog”A stated wish to play needs a lesson object such as the numbers, the slope or “it”. “Let me” and “can I” ask the tutor for a turn and pass.“I want to play with the numbers” · “Can I play with the pendulum?”

I also checked 139 archived learner messages in 126 distinct wordings. Comparing the current patterns with a checksummed copy of the original file found zero changes to cue kinds or target families.

What stays unknown

  • How often real learners get a control. Every count here is scripted or synthetic, and no build with these changes is in production.
  • Whether the two decision models differ on the slider request. One of the two turns never returned.
  • Whether controls mount in the classroom chat. They were exercised in offline replays at phone and desktop widths.
  • Whether a decision model would pass the v2.3 edit to the conveyor reply. No provider reviewed it.
  • Whether a code exercise keeps its difficulty through a retry. Two turns suggest it may not.
  • Whether the tutor writes fairer debugging exercises under v2.3.3. No tutor ran under it, and its proof covers one shape of program.
  • Whether a provider reviewer would catch a hidden bug elsewhere. Both missed the one example they were shown.
  • How the default classroom tutor behaves. No November study included it.
  • Whether learners use a control or learn more. No submission, retention or transfer data exists.
