Follow one learner request

Field guide by Claude Fable · 18 November 2026 · Keating teaching policy v2.3.3 to v2.3.8

Keating tries to notice what a learner wants to do, such as guess, vary or fix, without making them name a control. It should also step back when they ask for a short answer. Here is how to follow one request, and where the trail goes cold.

One request, three stops

  1. Learner wrote“Before you tell me, I’d like to guess how many layers it has.”
  2. Tutor draftedA working Question with layer counts to choose from, in draft 1.
  3. Learner receivedA sentence asking for the guess. Review faulted repetition around the field, and draft 3 went out.

The same request lost its Question a second time when a chooser preferred approved prose. After the fix, replaying the saved drafts publishes the Question in both turns. Fifteen other recorded outcomes are unchanged, and 21 counterexamples keep it out. This is an offline replay and nothing was rendered.

Trace it yourself

Open Settings → Diagnostics → Interface use, pick Final review · selected tutor replies, then Interface compiled, then dropped. Each row lists Compiled, Selected and Rendered, the private drafts, the released draft and the time to reply or withholding. Open the two losses

A model can ask through the read-only keating.openui_diagnostics WebMCP tool with {"view":"draft-loss","cue":"implicit-action","limit":5}, or the CLI:

rtk bun src/cli/main.ts openui-diagnostics web/src/keating/openui/examples/final-review.json --cue implicit-action --view draft-loss --limit 5

Native page requested means the tutor suggested a page. Only Preview card mounted or Ready frame in viewport means the learner was shown one.

Two live turns, both withheld

Headless, a typed correct guess was confirmed under both decision models. In the classroom, the same typed guess was sent twice, once on v2.3.7 and once on v2.3.8. The learner got a failure notice both times. First, v2.3.7.

  1. Learner typed“My guess is round, because three of the four tiles are round.”
  2. Tutor drafted“Exactly right”, with 3/4 and 1/4, in 175 words. It then began an interactive page nobody requested.
  3. Learner receivedA failure notice after 36 seconds. The page’s HTML hit the 3,072-token limit, so the draft never completed.

That tutor request also said the learner had not answered, although the plan knew they had. The study allowed no retry. Browser evidence · Terminal summary

v2.3.8 removed the wrong instruction, and one fresh classroom turn repeated the guess.

  1. Learner typedThe same guess, with the original Question still open.
  2. Tutor draftedThree complete drafts. The first added a new Question nobody requested. The second ran to 266 words and offered a simulation. The third was a correct 37-word confirmation that stopped.
  3. Learner receivedA failure notice after 29 seconds. All three drafts were reviewed and none passed. On the third, review was unsure whether it overclaimed mastery.

Where the trail goes cold

The inspector follows a control that went missing. It cannot yet follow something added that the learner did not ask for.

The v2.3.7 withheld row shows no detected cue, an unobserved page request and no review issues. Nothing names the page that consumed the answer. The v2.3.8 row does better. It lists seven review issues and a compiled Question that was never selected.

Neither row can say that a short, correct confirmation existed and was held. That is visible only in the sealed receipt.

Both reviewers also still hold an accurate correction of a wrong guess. Matched review outcomes

A declined quiz, now on the record

The irrigation learner asked to move a flow slider and added “No quiz, thanks.” All four released replies had a slider, so all four matched the request. Both unguided replies still opened their simulation instructions by telling the learner to predict. The diagnostics could not see that, because the row kept only the slider request.

New web turns now record two more facts, as categories with no words from either side. One is whether the learner explicitly declined to be tested. The other is whether the released reply carries a checkpoint signal in its simulation instructions, a question interface or its own text.

The rule is narrow on purpose. It reads explicit English wording and an instruction that opens a sentence. Optional, negated and quoted wording does not count.

It missed one thing I flagged by hand. The unguided MiniMax reply ends by asking the learner to report back so the tutor can see how they are thinking. That request sits mid-sentence, so the reply text is not signalled. The turn is still caught through its simulation instructions.

A reply with no signal is not shown to fit, and “no opt-out recognised” is not consent to be tested.

These are a few synthetic prompts and two live classroom turns. They show where controls worked and failed. They do not show production rates or that anyone learned more.
